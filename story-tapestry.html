Tapestry of Stars

In the aftermath of Erelyia’s fall, Karliah wakes wounded and alone — carrying questions that no one left alive can answer. An excerpt from Book I: Prologue and Chapters 1–3.

Prologue

The sparkling, starry sky above was a blood-red blur in the reflections of the sanguine pools below.

With one side of her face laying upon the bloodied tiles of the city’s courtyard, Karliah witnessed the world – the only one she had ever known – collapse; consumed by the shrieking of steel, the weeping of soon-to-be widows, and an ever-darkening sky. The taste of iron was sharp on her tongue. Everything had happened so quickly. It felt like only moments ago she was in her study, writing by the warmth of a candlelight, like any other night. Then, the alarm sounded. And a moment later, she was crossing swords with soldiers she had never seen. Now, her fingers left splotches of scarlet as they trailed across her armour, seeking the spot of warmth in her chest. They found only the cold bite of a blade.

An order called out from one of her soldiers, but she could not see them between the smoke and chaos. “Protect Lady Stellain!”

Looking down, Karliah’s chest rose and fell with half-breaths, cut even shorter as she eyed the sword half-buried in her pale body, sheened only by her own blood. There was no pain; only a mounting coldness. Instinct persuaded her hand to hold the steel and uproot the weapon from her flesh and armour. But she stayed her movement, as amid the hundreds of piercing cries and demanding shouts, Karliah suddenly heard her own name.

Then, the voice was by her side.

“Karliah?” Their hands held her from behind. “Breathe. You’re okay.” She started moving, her body being dragged by others that watched her with lifeless stares.

An explosion erupted from the other end of the courtyard. Karliah could only watch the war unfold, wincing as her and her men’s hold of Erelyia was being wrenched from them with every death. Between all of the bloodshed, Karliah eyed a silver-armoured woman stalk forth from the blur of battle, untouched by the war around her, sword still sheathed at her hip. Her gait was poised but pronounced by a touch of lethality; a kind Karliah had never seen before. The woman was at the head of the group that flanked her – all were different to the rest of the attacking soldiers, and yet, both parties brandished the same banner: an empty, dark-grey canvas, save for the black streaks spiralling to its centre. The strange woman and her party continued from the edge of the courtyard. None of the Erelyians who challenged her made it beyond her guard – seasoned soldiers, cut down effortlessly. Karliah’s hand went limp, loosening its hold on the hilt of her blade. The coldness was spreading, and her body was becoming numb.

“Stay with me.” The stranger’s voice was shallow, out of breath. “We’re almost—”

Karliah drifted in and out of touch with the world but witnessed – and felt – the impossibility of what happened next. Soldiers – ally and foe – came to a standstill, swords clattering as they fell from faltering fingers. All stared to the heart of the courtyard – to the woman in silver armour and her cadre standing together there. Karliah realised that the stranger who held her had stopped too. With a raised hand, the silver-armoured woman muttered a strange language, then the earth stirred, not only from below, but also above. The sky had been split asunder, scarred by a streak of darkness. Light spilled from its wound; an untameable torrent of celestial colours, crashing in on itself like a herd of wild beasts.

It continued falling, flailing against the illusory chains that tried to bind it, until those chains became real. The light had been reshaped, taking the form of a fragile stream, surrendering itself to the woman at the heart of the courtyard. The waking earth started to split, branching from where the woman stood. Karliah strained to see the rest, but her head went limp with weakness, and the girl could only stare into the red pools around her. She stared, searching for the reflection of a starry sky that was now gone.

Chapter One

Waking Wounds

Karliah felt the waking world with shut eyes. She listened to the woeful wind, shivered at the passing of the cold air it carried on its back. In the distance, lighting struck from dark clouds that blotted out the sky. Karliah felt whatever warmth she had within her wane, each breath becoming colder than the last.

The events of Erelyia were still a fresh wound on her mind. She had dreamt about them throughout the night, painfully reliving each moment. Each friend that had fallen. Each building that had been destroyed. The successes of Erelyia – a city that had withstood countless hardships for generations – had been undone in a single night, by an army no one had ever heard of. A part of Karliah believed that the dream she had was just that – a dream – until the wound at her midriff reminded her otherwise. She had dreamt of that too; the moment a sword had split her armour, tore through the leather beneath, and pierced her skin. It was warm with pain, and burned fiercely with every movement.

Karliah pulled the blanket closer, feeling herself slip into another dream – another reliving of that attack on Erelyia – until a nudge on her back roused her from sleep completely. She rolled over and opened her eyes – the sight sent her rearwards, slamming the back of her head against the wooden wall. She winced at the wound that had not yet completely healed, then looked to the horse, standing still and gazing with curiosity.

“He hasn’t moved from your side since you got here, you know.”

Karliah perched herself on an arm, feeling the soft scratches of dried straw. The horse pulled away as a man came into view. With an overcast sky, the barn was poorly lit, but Karliah could make out a hand running against the hazel coat of the steed between them.

“You’re awake, aren’t you?” The young man stepped into view and studied the girl with a furrowed brow. His clothes were weathered and well-worn, and yet, he could not have been older than her; a teen bordering on adolescence. He motioned to the horse beside him. “He got a name?”

Karliah did not remember riding into town, nor who had saved her during the attack. Her memories were a dreamlike blur, save for the pain that was serving as a constant reminder that all of this was real. She shifted her stare from the boy to the steed, then back to the boy who was still waiting for an answer. “I don’t know.”

A slow, awkward nod. “And you?”

“What about me?”

“You got a name?”

She took a moment, struggling to swallow with the dryness in her mouth. “Karliah.”

Another nod.

Then, the two of them were quiet. Karliah had too many questions on her mind, but was unsure of the stranger before her – unsure whether or not she could trust someone she had just met. She stared over her shoulder, looking beyond the gaps of the wood planks, witnessing the worsening weather outside.

“Here, you must be thirsty.” An outstretched hand offered a small wooden cup. “I’m Arvan, by the way.”

Karliah accepted, thanking him before taking a sip. “When did I get here?” She tried to gather her thoughts, “Where am I?”

The young stablekeeper moved his attention back to the horse. “This is Hearthmere.”

Hearthmere. Karliah had heard of the town; other soldiers talked about riding through it to continue further south, towards the coast. Erelyia must’ve been a fair distance away from here.

“You all arrived yesterday – in the middle of the night – and you’ve been here ever since. As for the man I don’t—”

“Who?” Karliah cut the boy’s words short.

The boy was puzzled, mirroring Karliah’s own expression, “My father says the rider who brought you here was in a rush to go before dawn. He stayed only to tend to that wound of yours. Didn’t say his name or where you rode from – he didn’t say much at all. Speaking of that…” he motioned to his body, “how is the wound? I heard it was really bad.”

Karliah touched the wraps gingerly. They were bandaged around her tightly, keeping pressure on her injury. There was a coldness there, as though the sword was still there. She did not know how she survived, not when so many others succumbed to wounds much less severe. She had to know who saved her, and how.

Arvan quickly eyed the front of the barn, then turned back to Karliah, his words now becoming a whisper. “Were you one of the soldiers at Erelyia?”

“I was.” Karliah was about to question the sudden lower tone. Were the soldiers she fought in Erelyia here too? Instead, she took the moment to search the area where she had slept for her belongings.

Surprise showed itself on the face of the young boy momentarily. He then realized Karliah was more than likely trying to find her armour. “Oh, your things are with my father,” Arvan stepped out of sight. A clammer of tools and wooden planks sounded, and soon enough the boy returned with something in his hands. “… but the rider told me to give you this.”

She took the sheathed object into her own hands.

“I—I have to go,” the boy started for the barn’s gates with a hurried pace. “My father works at the forge – it’s on the other end of town!” Then, he was gone.

Thunder growled in the distance. Karliah’s fingers began unlacing the delicate strings of the leather sheath. She unwrapped the tanned hide, layer by layer, until the steel revealed itself. The memory of Erelyia suddenly struck her vividly. The sword’s surface was dulled, dyed deep with what could only be her blood. The weapon fell from the fabric, clattering as it struck the stone floor. She wept, until her empty, shaking hands were filled with tears.

Rain came upon the town in violent torrents. Even in the middle of the night, Karliah was restless. Every time she shut her eyes, violent visions of Erelyia hounded her. The silver-armoured woman and her cadre. The stranger that saved her. The ritual of that strange light. None of it made any sense, and now she was alone in a town, with no one to give her answers to any of her questions. But what haunted her most was not just the killing and destruction, but what she saw at the end – what she still felt, even now.

Karliah closed her eyes once more. She pressed through the memories, endured them until all was dark. There was nothing except darkness. Then, from the stillness, a sphere of light appeared, like a single star against an unmoving, dark sky. It shone with colours so vivid; shades so deep. It was so bright, but not blinding. Karliah reached out, feeling the light on her skin. Its heat was familiar. She held the star, letting the warmth pass into her too. Her skin was illuminated as the light coursed down her arm, from within. It continued, slowly, moving like warm wax, until her body was glowing. Where there was once a star in the palm of her hand, now there was nothing. There was darkness again, only this time, it was alive. It surrounded her from all angles. Even the floor had become a shallow pool of inky darkness. Karliah was alone here, in this emptiness. Until the darkness spoke. With a thousand voices, but a single tongue, it uttered one word.

“Karliah.”

Karliah awoke, gasping for air, with one hand gripped tightly around the hilt of her sword. She was still in the barn, alone. For a moment – and ever so slightly – her hand appeared luminescent. She rested the sword by her side, then held her hand before her once again. The faint light upon her skin was only that of the moon that shone between a gap in the wooden planks.

“It’s only a dream.” Karliah laid down. She tried steadying her breathing. However, her name still reverberated within her mind.

Chapter Two

Hammer

The downpour continued late into the day. Even with the uninviting weather, there were still townspeople to be heard; vendors peddling their home-made wares, farmers shouting over one another.

Karliah strained as she pushed all of her weight into the heavy wooden doors. The hook of agony caught her in the ribs, dragging her to her knees with a choked cry. It took a moment until she was on her feet once more, steadying her breathing.

Beyond the barn’s gates, Karliah scouted the dirt path and dulled walls of the houses in sight. She glowered at the dreary day that only served to show the miserable state of the town. Everything was washed in dull shades of grey and dirtied by splotches of mud. She made her way to the street where most of the townsfolk voiced their wares to the passersby. Many of the stalls here were scarce, paling in comparison to Erelyia’s marketplace – or what it once was like. Karliah remembered them all. She would visit them every weekend, and her favourite had always been the vibrant-patterned tent that sold Sivaran sweets. She wondered if any of it had survived; if the old woman who was always there to welcome her had made it out too. In the distance, the toll of hammer and steel sung out, and Karliah let it guide her through the streets.

The girl arrived at the other end of town. With the forge in view, she approached the laboured breathing of bellows. A blacksmith was seated by the open pit of chars and cinders, spearing at the flames with unshapen metal. He turned, laying hammer upon steel against the anvil. The sound was a rhythm of violence. It vibrated in Karliah’s teeth and throbbed through her bones. There were decades of experience that perfected that constant rhythm.

“Excuse me,” Karliah stayed by the threshold separating the forge from the dirt path.

The blacksmith set aside the steel. He turned, swiping at his forehead beaded with sweat from the stinging heat and blackened by the thick smoke.

“I’m here for my things,” she studied the cluttered forge with quick glances, “your son sent me from the stables.”

“Mhm.” The acknowledgement was more of a low growl.

If she had to guess, the man had been about her father’s age, when he had still been alive. In a way, they looked alike, only that the man before her was much broader, body thick with thews. He fetched the bundle of her armour: chestplate, grieves, gauntlets, all appearing to glow in the firelight. The blacksmith handed her each item with ashy, calloused fingers, the kind only earned from a life’s worth of work.

“Thank you.”

Nothing. The man returned to the forge, tempering steel by hand and hammer.

Karliah waited for something else to be said, but nothing. Shifting the weight of the armour in her hands, she stepped into the old man’s view once more. “I’m Karliah, by the way.”

The man spared a moment for the girl, then his exhausted eyes were on the open pit of fire and coal once more. “Ergun.”

“Are there any other survivors? From Erelyia, I mean.”

The stony stare was kept on the smouldering rocks before him. “Erelyia?” He shifted the piece of steel to the anvil once more. The hammer rose and fell. “There is no Erelyia. Just a grave with walls.”

She pressed the man for answers. “Surely I was not the only one. Surely—”

“The sky split open, girl. I saw it.” he grunted, the rhythm of his hammer never breaking. “I saw things… things that looked like wolves but walked like men… hunting the roads.”

Karliah let the words linger between them for a moment. Even if one survivor had made it out of Erelyia, she needed to know. “There must have been someone else who survived.”

“If they did, they ain’t here.”

Something had happened here, in this town. Something that the man was keeping to himself. She was a moment from stepping away from the forge, until the smith spoke once more.

“Come.” The steel hissed as he dropped the piece of metal into the barrel of water. Setting his tools to the side, the blacksmith got up and started for the door at the front of the house. “I’ll tell you, but not here.”

Sat by the hearth, Karliah embraced the fireplace, letting it weave its warmth through her ashen hair. She admired the adornments scattered throughout the blacksmith’s small home. Most were trinkets, pieces of jewellery dulled by a coat of dust.

Ergun brought a pot from the kitchen and set it to rest above the fire. The cracking wood broke the silence between them. “There were more survivors, soon after you rode in.” He took a seat across from the girl. “The townsmen turned them away.”

Karliah turned to the old man. “Why?”

Ergun’s stare did not stray from the hearth. “A group of men passed through town. Soldiers – the kind I had not seen before. They said any survivors from the city would be killed, along with whoever was helping them.”

The two were quiet for a moment, and Karliah was now looking to the hearth. The flames did not thrash violently, nor did they spout black clouds of smoke. They were not yearning to consume the world; not like the ones in Erelyia had.

“You were asleep at the barn.” His stone-cold features did not change, even so close to the heat. “The rest of the townsfolk must’ve not known.”

“You don’t trust them?”

A muscle flinched at the blacksmith’s jaw. “No.”

Karliah did not press for answers. She studied what she could see of the home. Even with all the ornaments, the room felt empty; incomplete, in a way. She sensed the same in the man beside her. Reaching for the small table between them, the girl plucked a small ring from the assortment of jewellery. It was made of a stone she had never seen before, crudely handcrafted, yet, beautiful.

“I made them for her.” The blacksmith peered around the room, occasionally pausing for a moment, as though reliving memories in the mementos he eyed. “Everything here was for her.”

Karliah set the ring down, then waited a moment before looking at the old man. “What happened?”

“Bandits. They took everything,” his shoulders sank at the words. “Even her.”

Karliah had heard about attacks like these. The city would send patrols of soldiers to aid the towns and villages in the surrounding region, but they were scarce, and bandits often took the opportunity to strike when their prey was weakest.

“I couldn’t save her alone, and no one here would help me. After all of these years. After everything I have done for these people, I—” He bit back the tears that were welling.

The fireplace crackled.

“I’m sorry.” Karliah did not know what else to say.

Ergun did not speak for a while. He stirred the broth, rousing streaks of smoke from the pot that filled the room with warmth.

The hot breaths of the forge were quick to greet the girl as she stepped outside. Tightening the straps of her chest piece, Karliah approached Ergun, already awake and busy with his craft. The weather remained unchanged; the town was still downtrodden by a rain refusing to leave. She looked back to the forge, finding the old man on his feet, moving between the equipment.

“Ergun,” Karliah called above the bellows.

The old man turned, noting the girl.

“I’m headed off. Thank you for your help.”

The appreciation merited a nod from the blacksmith. “Take care of yourself, Karliah.”

The town was dyed by the dreary presence of winter. The path back to the barn was disfigured, murky water filling the footprints of the muddy terrain. Even fewer people were out on the street today. Most of the stalls were shut, and those that were open were bare. Karliah felt the weight of her leathers swell as she continued through the streets, heavy with rainfall. As she approached a bend just before the barn, the girl noted several coated men loitering at the corner, all in conversation. Their drunken laughs cried out like howls. Karliah’s approach hushed the pack, and she felt their eyes track her. Beneath the draped cape, she placed a hand upon the hilt at her side. One of them spat at her feet, and the others snickered amongst themselves. She rounded the bend, ignoring the scoffs that trailed her.

The stable’s gate was slightly ajar, and the light from within was an inviting sight. Karliah was eager to get out of the rain, but she paused at the doors, seeing something further along the empty street. Another man, standing alone. They wore a black coat, glossy in the downpour. Karliah might have mistaken the man for a shadow, had she not noticed the red ribbon tied to his sword’s hilt, flailing in the wind. The weapon was sheathed on his back, and yet, she could tell from the handle alone that the weapon was hefty. They did not move. Nor did Karliah. It was difficult to see through the haze of rain, but Karliah felt the man staring back at her.

“You’re back!” A youthful voice called out from within the barn.

Karliah stiffened for a split second, but then slipped between the gates to greet Arvan within the stables, tending to two horses. She eyed the back of the barn, seeing the horse that she had supposedly ridden into town with. He was still waiting where she had been asleep.

“Yes, but I’m going out of town for a while.”

“That’s perfect!” The young stablekeeper was moving, reaching for a saddle that hung on the wall.

“How so?” Karliah was curious at the sight of the boy’s eagerness.

“Because…” He rushed across the room to introduce someone. “We want you to take us with you!”

Beside Arvan was another boy, both were about the same age as each other. Compared to the stable boy, he was much more timid, standing shyly with pressed lips. He motioned an awkward wave to Karliah.

“I don’t think so—”

“But please! Neither of us have seen the city, and we both really want to.” He nudged his friend, who only nodded in agreement.

“It’s too dangerous.”

“That’s why we have you with us! You even said it yourself, you’re a soldier.” There was no sign of the boy giving up.

Karliah went to protest once more, but this boy – and his father – had saved her life. Without their aid, she would not be here. “Okay, but only if you listen to me when we’re out there. The moment I tell you to go home, you go.”

“Of course,” Arvan was bright-eyed, gleaming with joy. Even a faint smile revealed itself on the friend’s face. “Also I didn’t think you’d be back so soon. So, I’ll put these on again.” He moved to the horse by the end of the barn, with a saddle in hand.

Karliah nodded her thanks. She stepped halfway out of the gateway, squinting against the small, sharp bites of the rain. The drunkards from earlier were still by the corner, but as she searched the other end of the street for the cloaked stranger, there was nothing to be found except quiet and blur.

“You should give him a name, you know.” Arvan patted the bridge of the steed’s nose. “They say it’s bad luck to ride on a horse without one.”

Karliah approached, and mounted the saddle, then eyed the steed for a moment.

“I’ll name him Hammer.”

Chapter Three

Born of the Dark

With the sun tucked between the ridges of distant mountains, Karliah and Hammer ventured further into the fields, away from the town. In tow, Arvan and his friend were mounted on their own steeds. The sky was a bruised blush of colour, and the view caught the girl’s eye, time and time again. Having seldom spent time beyond Erelyia’s white walls, Karliah had never quite beheld the view of the world like this. She stared to the open plains of Vizien – a region often said to be amongst the most beautiful across all of Haeryn. She was imagining the life she had never lived, but still could. The thought of straying from this path was tempting. She could ride elsewhere and start anew, but her mind would not find rest while there were more questions that needed answers. She continued to the city of Erelyia.

Hammer’s canter kept steady throughout the ride, and soon enough, the girl’s eyes narrowed on the city that came into view. From behind, she heard murmurs exchanged between the two boys, no doubt in awe of the sight. However, Karliah could only hold back her tears. The once-white walls of Erelyia had been blackened by the blight of war. She spotted no sign of survivors beyond them, for all the buildings were dark, even as daylight drained from the sky.

Before continuing onwards, Karliah reined the steed to a standstill. She waited for Arvan and his companion to reach her. Ergun had warned her of bandits in the area, but atop the mare, she studied the steppes behind them and found no sign of them.

The three of them stood in silence for a moment, but it was Arvan who spoke out first.

“I can’t believe it. I’ve never seen anything like this.”

Karliah smiled at the boy’s excitement. She wished that she could share his sentiment.

As the three continued on, closer to the quiet city, Karliah realised a change in Hammer’s trot. The steady, soft pats on grassy fields were replaced by dull thuds on metal. Then, the living fetor of death announced itself. She felt the foul air stick to her skin and nest within her hair. First, it was the murmur of flies, followed by the whispers of vermin; both filling the stilled quiet of the battlefield.

The joy from Arvan’s face had faded. “Karliah…”

With each stride, Karliah caught glimpses of rodents hiding themselves under armour and spotted others continuing their feasts within the festered, fleshy cavities of corpses. She looked to the bodies that filled the clearing, most half-buried beneath mud, and the rest, even further below. Most of the faces that stared back were strangers, but Karliah had recognised some, more than she had wished she would. They brought back memories of time together; a world that was once her dream, now twisted into a nightmare.

She turned to the boys who were still in tow, “You two should go.”

A moment of hesitation revealed itself on the stable boy’s face. As for the awkward boy next to him, Karliah had overheard him ask his friend to go home several times now. However, Arvan’s stubbornness refused to yield to his curiosity.

“But I—”

“I said go. Now.” She ordered; with the same sharp tone she had once used to command her soldiers. Her voice was still raw from the shouting of that night.

Arvan was reluctant, but continued no further.

This was for the best, Karliah thought to herself. She did not know what was waiting beyond this field, beyond those walls.

She led Hammer the rest of the way on foot, until they were where the gates of Erelyia once stood. All that remained of them now was debris: bits of wood and bone jutting out from mounds of muddied ash. The girl started for the city’s lightless streets, until Hammer’s lead became taut. Turning, Karliah met the steed’s stubborn stare.

“Come,” she tugged at the leather line.

Hammer shook his head in protest, stomping the floor with its front hooves.

She searched the streets again, straining to see a sign of movement within the stillness. No one was here. She stared at the shadows until they appeared to stir. Then she realised the horse was not concerned by who, but rather, what was before them. Karliah peered to the ground by the gateway, eyeing the strange seams that seemed etched into the earth. She crouched before the battered entrance of the broken city, fingers following the sprawling grooves of the ground that glowed with faint luminosity. There was something in them, staining the cobble and dirt like blood, except, it was alive, swirling with shades of blue and violet, among others. She held her hand above it, feeling a weak warmth, akin to coals of a doused campfire. Inching closer to the coloured veins, the girl swore she could hear something – a thrum – so slightly exuded by them.

Continuing deeper into the darkened streets, Karliah discovered more traces of the light. Most ran along the exposed earth in short rivulets, while others wrapped around the walls of the city’s razed remnants. It appeared as though all the instances of light were leading somewhere, further into the darkness. Karliah followed, rounding a corner, only to find more bodies here than in the streets before. Many wore the same armour as her, others she did not recognise. She strode the path leading to the city’s courtyard, the place of their last stand against the attackers. The veins that wove between the bodies were brighter here, and the hum in the air had deepened. Karliah stayed the steed as she reached the end of the street, standing at the edge of the circular clearing. The heart of Erelyia.

Much of the courtyard had been wrecked, not by the war, but by the crater where the silver-armoured woman had performed that ritual. Karliah eyed the colours, tracing them to the crater at the centre of the courtyard; the source of where all of the strange light seemed to be birthed from. She toed between the bodies until she stood where the silver-armoured woman had on the night of Erelyia’s fall, not noting the blood-red trail she made with each step. Staring within the rounded ditch, the illumination was materialised, taking the shape of shattered glass. In that moment, Karliah pieced together the fragmented memories of the event. The colours she had seen up until this point were akin to the ones from the ritual, moments before she slipped out of consciousness. Only now, the colours were not within the sky, but all around her, fallen upon the city and bleeding into the earth.

Looking up, Karliah found a pool of darkness stirring far above her, a body of emptiness between the stars. It swirled slowly in unpatterned loops, like a disturbed puddle of ink. A sudden dread gathered within the girl; the presence – whether from the darkness in the sky or the colour around her – was most potent here, at the centre of the courtyard. Karliah knelt down, reaching for the pieces of light. Then, there was a whisper.

“What is it?”

The girl whirled on her feet, unsheathing her sword in a swift movement, only to find the face at the end of her blade was none other than the stable boy.

She lowered her sword with a drawn-out breath. “What are you doing here?” She looked over the boy’s shoulder to see his friend gazing at the strange lights. “Both of you.”

Immediately, Arvan was voicing his pleas with her. “Please! We only wanted to see what this was.” He motioned to the lights surrounding them.

“And now you have, so it’s time to go.”

“But what is it?”

Her patience with the boy was waning. “Arvan, I do not know. Nor do I have the time—” A sharp, violent rush of air tore through the clearing, cutting her sentence short. The world had gone strangely still. The strange hum of the light was silent. Whatever caused that sudden sound came from the other end of the cobbled courtyard.

She searched the surroundings. There was only stillness. Moving away from the boys, she stalked through the softly-lit courtyard, moving between the rubble and bodies. Her breaths rose and fell unevenly. Then, a low-slung shape flickered across the periphery of her vision. It moved with terrifying speed; far too fast for its size.

Karliah halted. She felt the weight of something strange. She looked to the buildings, then between the trees of the courtyard’s garden. There was nothing. Turning around, she spotted Arvan and his friend still waiting, until her eyes adjusted on the figure just beyond them. It loomed half-hidden in the shadow, large and heavy-set. Even at a distance, the whites of its eyes were unnaturally bright. She opened her mouth to shout, but dread had already seized her. The coldness it brought stiffened her completely.

The boy beside Arvan noticed. He glanced behind him, and the shout that followed split the silence.

“A—ARVAN—”

With a concussive snap of muscle, the creature uncoiled; its heavy-chested frame surged forward. In a moment, it erased the distance between itself and the boy like an arrow loosed from a siege bow. It did not just strike the boy. A sudden, violent blur of motion, and he was gone, replaced by a spray of red on the grey stones.

Arvan broke into a sprint, but the beast was already upon him. The breaking of bones sounded at the impact, and the beast’s claws threw sparks against the cobbles. Karliah had never seen something like it.

The white eyes turned to her direction. Then, they lowered, until the beast’s body was perched on its palms, like an animal poised at its prey. Karliah’s steps were slow as she distanced herself, not breaking away from the white-eyed stare. It did not move, staying in its unsettling stillness. Her boot caught itself on a body, and a panicked huff of air escaped the girl as she looked away to not misplace her next step. Her head snapped up at the thuds that hounded her way. The creature charged into the moonlit clearing, revealing its beastly body. It bore feline-like fangs, chipped and curving downwards from its maw.

Karliah ran for the closest end of the courtyard. Hammer was nowhere to be found. The drumming footfalls gathered, louder. They were replaced by sudden snapping of bones: corpses caught in the beast’s warpath. She waited until it was near, then tumbled to the side. The attempt was made a moment too soon. It slammed into her, and the sword was sent clattering on the stones. The beast’s claws carved into the paving to stop its momentum. On her back, she beheld the creature’s misshapen, blackened body, watched it rise and fall with breaths of feral ferocity. Its eyes were upon her, and its approach was predatory, drawn out to savour the girl’s fear. Her boots scuffled on the cobbles as she shuffled away, moving between the bodies. Then, the creature surged, and a violent crack reverberated through the stones. The sound of steel on bone. There it was once more – the red ribbon.

Karliah watched as the man wrenched the weapon free, scattering black blood across the tiles. He swung again, groaning through gritted teeth. The impact reverberated through the stones, and the beast’s cry that followed was deafening.

The girl was already on her feet. Bits of sharp debris bit from beneath her boots as she started for a nearby alleyway that ran between the city’s church and several storied houses. She entered the backstreet and a violent slam sounded from behind, shuddering the floor and sending her off-balance. The beast was fuelled with frustration as it slammed itself into the building again, sending loose stones falling from the walls. It tore at the buildings, cleaving a way through to the girl through the too-narrow path. Karliah spotted an opening at the church’s foundation, but as she pressed herself between the gap, the creature’s claws flayed the leathers from her back. Its roar was a muffled cry as the girl stumbled onto the cold cobble.

From within, she heard the man and beast clash once again. Even numbed by the adrenaline, she felt the warmth of blood flow down her back. She passed through an archway leading to another room, then another, breathing deep as her face grew flush with heat. Here, beneath the chapel, these rooms were unkempt as though untouched for hundreds of years. Small slits in the walls were filled with scrolls and books, many of which were withered from time. She paused for a moment, bracing herself on one of the archways to steady her breathing. A roar sounded from above her. As her eyes adjusted to the darkness, Karliah spotted a subtle glow beneath a door to the side of the room. It felt familiar, as did the hum that gathered in the air. She made her way into the room beyond, drawn like a moth to a flame.

The rounded space of the room was empty, save for a vast, but shallow well at the centre of the room. The starry colours from within cascaded over the well’s rim and flowed onto the stone steps that encircled it. The light reflected off the vaulted ceiling, where dyed glass had been hand-cut and set into the stone. She approached, focused on the colours that flowed out from the well. It flowed like water, but as it landed on the last step, it turned to mist, soon disappearing into the air. It was the light.

Karliah bucked, toeing on the brink of consciousness. She toppled at the base of the well, spitting blood on the stones. She hauled her bloodied body over each of the steps. The light called to her, a song in the silence of her suffering, and she did not question it. Not as she drifted atop the celestial pool, weightlessly. Her body started to sink, further and further. With closed eyes, she watched colours take shape from darkness. There were moments of complexity, then moments of nothingness. Karliah surrendered herself to it completely, witnessing the visions unfold, brighter the further into darkness she drifted.

Then, it ended.

Darkness was all there was.

Until a sound. Then, footsteps. They became louder, closer, until she felt herself being pulled from the deep to the surface. The girl gasped for air until her chest could rise no more. As she opened her eyes, a man stood towering over her, waiting. He had been cut all over, and his leathers were blood-soaked. His exhausted eyes broke away from hers as he went to retrieve his sword that had been tossed aside. Its sheen was blackened and marred, by his fight with the beast, no doubt.

The body of the felled beast was not far from the man, off to the side of the room. It was headless and still.

“Get up,” he ordered.

“I…” Karliah no longer felt the hurt in her back, or her chest. Her body was not as weak as it had been only moments ago. She touched the spots of where her wounds had been, but the pain was gone. Then, she noticed that the ceiling had collapsed, with blocks of cobble scattered throughout the room. The well was positioned directly underneath the centre of the courtyard.

“Here.” He offered the girl her sword. As he stood there, he studied the girl with a furrowed brow, realising what had happened to her too.

Staying in tow until they reached the courtyard, Karliah paused, spotting Arvan and his friend, or what remained of them.

“Call your horse.”

“Why?”

The man turned to her, face unchanged from that expression of exhaustion and displeasure, but there was something more in his eyes now. Unease.

He looked at the dry, empty well, then the darkened buildings where the bleeding veins of light had now faded into the dull grey stone. He kicked a loose cobble into the crater, listening to the hollow clatter. Turning around to look at her once more, the swordsman’s gaze lingered on the fresh, pink skin where her fatal wound had been only moments ago.

“None of this is natural,” he said, his voice flat. “I don’t know what just happened here, but we need answers. We will go speak to her.”

Karliah studied the area around them. The hum was gone. The magic was dead.

“To who?” she asked.

“The Raven.”
